### [OpenAI Essay Argues Perplexity Tracks Model Intelligence](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

OpenAI published an essay arguing that perplexity, a language-model prediction metric, tracks model intelligence. The supplied analysis characterizes the piece as a conceptual argument rather than a new model release or an independently measured result; no essay text was available to verify its specific evidence or definitions.

hackernews · acossta · Nov 22, 17:06 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601525)

**「What perplexity measures」** Perplexity is the exponentiated average cross-entropy loss a language model incurs when predicting each next token on held-out text: a perfect predictor scores 1, a uniform-random predictor roughly the vocabulary size, and lower is better. It had long served as a standard pre-training diagnostic before Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI&\#x27;s chief scientist, published the essay arguing that it should also become a shared metric for comparing model developers and informing common safety standards \[tool-2-1\].

**「Community Discussion」** Commenters criticized the essay for not defining perplexity, with one explaining it as next-token surprise bounded by 1 at best and vocabulary size at worst. Others debated why cross-entropy loss still appeared predictive amid reinforcement-learning training, and one linked the prediction-as-compression argument to earlier work.

### [O&\#x27;Reilly essay asks what happened to Silicon Valley](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/what-happened-to-silicon-valley/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

An O&\#x27;Reilly Radar essay titled &quot;What Happened to Silicon Valley?&quot; argues that the industry&\#x27;s direction is set less by a natural market order than by mechanism design — the rules and incentives its architects choose — and asks what technology should collectively be optimizing for. As readers quote it, the essay frames mechanism design as &quot;reverse game theory&quot;: rather than asking the rules of the game now being played, it asks &quot;what game ought we to be playing?&quot; and answers &quot;the game of human flourishing.&quot; The piece is an argument essay about market incentives, corporate goals, and platform economics, not a report of a new product, policy change, or measured result.

hackernews · greenyouse · Nov 22, 19:20 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602947)

**「Background」** Mechanism design—often described as reverse game theory—asks what rules would produce a desired outcome rather than only analyzing the game already in play. O&\#x27;Reilly&\#x27;s essay uses that frame to examine how Silicon Valley&\#x27;s market incentives and corporate goals shaped the industry&\#x27;s evolution.

**「Impact」** For engineers and product managers, the essay&\#x27;s argument implies that a system&\#x27;s optimization target — ad revenue, engagement, or another metric — is a design choice made by identifiable people rather than an emergent property of the market, and can therefore be specified and challenged before deployment.

**「Community discussion」** Commenters largely endorsed the essay&\#x27;s framing while disagreeing about causes: one recalled Hal Varian saying only about 6% of Google search result pages carried advertising, presented as evidence that early tech created more value than it captured, while another argued the ZIRP era let companies grow &quot;to grotesque proportions&quot; and that AI spending is now cannibalizing core businesses. Others said the essay&\#x27;s key point — that mechanism design should ask what humanity ought to optimize for — is buried near the end, and that the industry&\#x27;s &quot;machine&quot; should have its designers held accountable rather than be treated as a natural phenomenon.

### [When in Doubt, Flail: Deliberate Experimentation Under Uncertainty](https://seangoedecke.com/when-in-doubt-flail-about/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

rss · Sean Goedecke · Nov 23, 00:00

**「Background」** When an engineer lands in a situation with no clear guidance and no obvious path forward, the instinct is to wait until the picture becomes clearer. Sean Goedecke argues that instinct is usually wrong — and that the faster route to understanding is to deliberately flail.

**「Solution」** His method has three parts: try many different things to make direct contact with reality, watch feedback closely — both from more experienced colleagues and from measurable results like latency or user metrics — and ruthlessly drop anything that isn&\#x27;t drawing a positive response. Volume matters for three reasons: most plans will be bad, so ten attempts beat one; a spread of attempts prevents over-investment in any single plan; and more attempts mean more exposure to reality. He applies this to product work \(at GitHub Models most of his features went nowhere, but running AI inference from a GitHub Actions token proved sticky and seeded &quot;Continuous AI&quot;\), to engineering \(making speculative changes — even having AI agents generate five refactors to skim — rather than fully mapping a system too large and fast-changing to comprehend\), and to organizational problems where nobody can articulate the fix. Organizational flailing is the riskiest: dropping a failing plan builds trust, while ignoring feedback directs others&\#x27; frustration at you. The name is deliberate, since it feels like repeated failure from the inside even when managers see proactive experimentation.

**「Takeaway」** Under genuine ambiguity, rapid low-commitment experimentation with tight feedback loops beats waiting for clarity — and the unpleasant sensation of flailing is a normal cost rather than evidence you are in trouble, provided you abandon what doesn&\#x27;t work.

### [US Tech Selloff Prompts Asian Market Stabilization Moves](https://news.bjd.com.cn/2026/11/22/12039852.shtml) ⭐️ 9.0/10
